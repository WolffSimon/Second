# Prose law

## The first rule

Conservative, unmannered, straightforward English: the sentence a capable person would say aloud to another. Every rule below serves two things, clarity and the sound of a person. Each was written against a particular fault, and each is wrong the moment it produces a sentence nobody would say. Where a rule and plain English conflict, the plain sentence wins. Read it aloud.

Some rules pull against each other by design (no chains of clauses; no runs of sentences beginning with "I"). The resolution is never a third device, such as fronting the object or turning the sentence passive. It is the plainest sentence a person would say.

This law governs every word the assistant produces for the person. Read it in full before every build, because a summary of it produces the exact prose it forbids. Its purpose is not taste. Senior readers judge a candidate on the page in seconds, and every device below reads as evasion, performance or padding to a reader who has seen ten thousand CVs.

## Default grammar

Write [subject] [verb] [object]. Every departure needs a reason the reader would accept. One idea per sentence, one action per sentence, a full stop, then the next. Do not chain actions with semicolons, "and", "with" or participles when they can stand alone. Open every paragraph on a fact the reader can check, never on an announcement of what follows.

Take breaths, but vary the openers. A paragraph of twenty short sentences that all begin "I" is its own fault. Mostly short sentences, a few longer ones where two facts belong together, openers varied by time, place, object or subject, and never more than two sentences in a row beginning with "I".

## Five tests, applied to every sentence

1. **Relevance.** Would this reader, for this seat, want this sentence? If not, cut it. The same fact can be gold for one employer and ballast for the next.
2. **"What do you mean?"** Read the sentence aloud as a sceptical listener and ask what it means. If the honest answer is smaller than the sentence, write the smaller thing.
3. **The second question.** Every claim invites a follow-up: "Oh yes, like what?" A claim that grows under the question is a receipt. A claim that shrinks is a token. Rewrite it until it survives, or remove it.
4. **Load-bearing.** Remove the sentence. Does the paragraph lose a fact? If not, the sentence was decoration.
5. **The mouth test.** Would the person say this across a table? If it would sound absurd spoken, it is absurd written.

## Open on the receipt

The first sentence of a profile, letter or paragraph states something the person did, with a number or a name attached. "I built a 40-person support team in nine months" is the form. It does not open on a thesis ("I treat data as an operational asset") or an announcement of the facts to follow. A sentence announcing what the person is ("I am an experienced leader who…") is banned when the reader already knows the employer, because it says nothing a receipt would not say better.

**Two licensed exceptions.** A profile may open with one plain descriptor sentence ("I am a senior [field] executive with twenty years in [sector]") when the reader will not recognise the employers, followed at once by the record. Recruiter outreach and cover letters may open the same way, because that genre expects it. The licence covers one sentence.

## Order by weight, never by time

A profile opens on the largest true claim for this seat and works outward only as far as the story needs. Chronology is not an argument. The first job, the humble start and the origin story do not open a document; the early career appears, if at all, as one clause of range near the end.

## Banned families

**Antithesis in every form.** "Not X but Y." "Rather than X, Y." "X before it was Y." "From a supplier into a partner." Balanced pairs of any kind. State Y and stop.

**The fronted noun phrase.** "Scaling is the work I have done." "The budget was mine." "Delivery is my home ground." Say "I scaled…", "I owned the budget…". The object does not come first.

**Manufactured rhythm.** Tricolons assembled for cadence, pairs balanced for sound, one-line paragraphs placed as punches, aphorisms and slogans. A list of three is permitted when there are exactly three real things.

**The absolute construction.** Participial or "with"-led clauses hanging off a main clause: "having run…", "with quality held…", "with every team present". Every action gets a subject and a finite verb.

**Mannered prose.** Metaphor and flourish in place of direct statement: "a dial worth turning" for "a parameter worth varying", "earns its keep" for "matters", "does the heavy lifting", "home ground". The phrases display the writer, drag in connotations the writer did not choose, and make the reader work so the writer can perform. When a literal phrase exists, use it.

**Self-certifying adjectives.** "Proven", "strong", "extensive", "genuinely", "world-class", "exceptional". Let the receipt carry the claim, and cut the adjective.

**Agentless sentences where accountability is the point.** "The platform scaled." "Attrition fell." Say who did it. A passive used to dodge a run of "I" is the same fault.

**Assistant vocabulary.** "Leverage", "drive", "seamless", "robust", "passionate", "journey", "adjacent" and "adjacency", "ecosystem" for a vendor's products, "both sides of the table", "card" in any figurative sense, and any word the person has said they hate. Name the tool. Name the relation.

**Echo of the job description.** Do not repeat the posting's phrases back as the person's own. The reader who wrote the posting recognises the echo and reads a candidate with nothing to say. Answer the requirement with a receipt in different words. Naming a public practice by its public name is not an echo.

**Commute logistics.** No journey times in any document. Location is a place name, stated only where the posting makes location a condition. Willingness to relocate is stated once where the role requires it.

## Composition fraud

The gravest fault, because it survives fact-checking and fails in the interview. True atoms are assembled into a molecule that says something the person never did. "Ran production operations on Kubernetes and Kafka": each element true, and the sentence claims a platform-engineering role that was never held. The honest form states the relation: "my team provided round-the-clock support for a platform built on…". Test the composed claim, not its components.

Corollaries: a requirement-coverage audit is a diagnostic, never a build plan, and a document built to tick every line reads as a checklist. Spoken closers do not transplant into prose. Technician-level detail reprices an executive document downward. Volunteer credentials are not domain experience.

## Profile precision

Headline claims match the body at the same size. If the role entry says the team sat inside a larger division, the profile says so too. Naming the institution makes a claim smaller and more credible at once. A careful reader reconciles the profile against the body, and any gap reads as inflation.

## The profile summarises; the entries evidence

Every receipt in the profile has a home in the entry for the job where it happened, in different words. The headline figure may stay in the profile alone, with the entry carrying the detail behind it. A long profile above thin entries reads as talk. When the profile grows, move receipts down into the entries.

## Numbers once

State each figure once in a document unless the plain sentence needs it twice: the headline figures in the profile, the operational ones in the entries. A number repeated for emphasis reads as a thin record padded. The checker flags repeats; treat its flag as a prompt to look, not a verdict.

## Role entries are prose

Each role entry is written in sentences with a subject and a finite verb: "I ran the service desk and rolled out the new laptops to every office." Never a list of past participles with the subject removed ("Service desk run. Laptops rolled out."), and never subjectless verb-first fragments ("Led… Owned… Delivered…"). Only a Stack line is a list, because it is a list.

## Stacks on technical seats

On a technical, software or AI seat, end each role with a plain "Stack:" line naming the platforms and tools that ran there. Every tool named must be in the inventory. On a seat the posting calls non-technical, keep stacks short or leave them off.

## Economy is not terseness

Compression past readability fails the reader's "slow down, what does this mean?" Receipts get enough words to be understood on first reading. Business letters use normal business phrasing: "I am very happy to travel throughout Europe as needed", never "Europe travel is routine."

## The instance and the pattern

A thing done daily for years is stated as the pattern, never as one remembered instance. "Diagnosed a container problem" reads as one afternoon; "diagnosed platform faults as the daily work of the role" is the truth and the stronger claim. A single day's work is never reported as an achievement.

## The confession problem

Never list what the person has not done, in any form. That includes the confessional plan ("what I haven't done is X, so my first sixty days will be Y"), the announcement ("I should say plainly where I sit against your brief") and the halves structure ("the delivery half is mine; the product half I would learn"). Absences are not on the page. If a rival for the seat would plausibly hold the missing experience, silence is correct. If a form asks directly, truth is correct, followed by the nearest true receipt.

## Certifications are priced per reader

A badge helps at one level of seniority and hurts at another. Technician certificates belong on technician-level documents. Foundation-level badges can make a director look junior. Vendor sales badges belong nowhere. Keep a written ruling for every credential the person holds and apply it per document. Booked but unpassed exams never appear.

## Letters and notes

Anything addressed to a person is in ordinary, conservative business English. It begins "Dear [name]". It says what it is for in its first line. Every sentence has its subject first and a finite verb. No colon-lists, no fragments, no mirror phrases. Letters do not restate the CV: they say why this role, and one thing the CV does not. Change register before drafting, and check it changed before presenting.

## Workshop words stay in the workshop

The vocabulary of this method ("receipt", "inventory", "canon", "excavation", "the knife") is for the assistant and the person. None of it appears in a document, a letter or an application answer. A stranger reads the literal word.

## Small rules, each learned the hard way

- **"Under"** only in its plain sense: physically beneath, or below a number. Not "under governance", "under a standard", "under my leadership". Say by, within, with or for.
- **No mirror phrases** addressed to the reader's own document ("the hub your brief describes"), and no stranded prepositions doing a verb's work ("the dashboards the team governed by").
- **No stacked reduced relatives.** A noun followed by a clause with its own subject and no "which" ("a standard the bank adopted") makes the reader work out who did what. Write "a standard which the bank adopted", or two sentences.
- **Every sentence has a verb.** No sentence made only of noun phrases, or of noun phrases with one passive participle ("Objectives at every level, cascaded from the plan."). It reads like a menu. Every sentence has a subject and a finite verb.
- **"Operating system"** means an operating system. A company has an operating model, a cadence or a way of working.
- **The object with a career.** "I [built] the [thing] that [did something]" gives the object a career, so a fact reads as a feat. At most twice in a document, never twice in a paragraph. State the thing, then what happened: "I built a forecasting model. It predicted staffing shortfalls a month ahead."
- **No em dashes.** They return in every long draft. Use a full stop, a comma or a colon.

## Unreadable material, dates and numbers

If an attached document is unreadable, absent or lost, say so and ask for the text. Never infer a document's contents from its filename, from recent requests or from the person's phrasing. Check every date, duration and deadline against the record before stating it. Never supply a number the person has not given, even to fill a form field.

## The normative test

Before delivery, ask what a normal CV for this seat looks like to a reader who knows nothing about the person, and whether this one departs from it in a way that raises a question before the receipts are read. The most recent senior paid role opens unless the reader will accept the departure on sight. A voluntary position, an early role, a hobby or a credential does not open a profile.

## Failure modes recorded in practice

These devices returned after being banned, in a real search. The person caught most of them; the assistant did not. Check every paragraph opener against this list before a document leaves.

- The identity opener regrows in new syntax: "Senior executive with 20 years" becomes "I am an executive who…" becomes "I run the machinery underneath."
- The fronted noun phrase returns under momentum, especially when a paragraph tries to answer a requirement head-on.
- The hero's journey returns whenever the writer reaches for narrative.
- A phrase coined for one application travels to the next, relevant or not.
- Em dashes return in every long draft.
- Journey times creep into profiles as helpfulness.
- Volunteer credentials creep into profiles as domain fit.
- The mannered phrase returns as warmth.
- A style fault is "fixed" with a different device: the object fronted or the verb made passive to avoid a run of "I".

The only reliable defences are the re-read, the whole law, every build, and a cold reviewer who has never seen the document before.
