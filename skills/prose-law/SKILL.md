# Prose law

This law governs every word the assistant produces for the person. It is read in full before every build, because a summary of it produces the exact prose it forbids. Its purpose is not taste. Senior readers price a candidate on the page in seconds, and every device below reads as evasion, performance or padding to a reader who has seen ten thousand CVs.

## Default grammar

Write [subject] [verb] [object]. Every departure needs a reason the reader would accept. One idea per sentence. One subject per sentence where possible. Full sentences, not serial short clauses hung on a colon. Open every paragraph on a fact the reader can check, never on an announcement of what follows.

## Five tests, applied to every sentence

1. **Relevance.** Would this reader, for this seat, want this sentence? If not, cut it.
2. **"What do you mean?"** Read the sentence aloud as a sceptical listener and ask what it means. If the honest answer is smaller than the sentence, write the smaller thing.
3. **The second question.** Every claim invites a follow-up. "You built the platform?" "What did you personally decide?" A sentence that cannot survive its second question is rewritten until it can, or removed.
4. **Load-bearing.** Remove the sentence. Does the paragraph lose a fact? If not, the sentence was decoration.
5. **The mouth test.** Would the person say this across a table? If it would sound absurd spoken, it is absurd written.

## Open on the receipt

The first sentence of a profile, letter or paragraph states something the person did, with a number or a name attached. It does not state what the person is. "I am an experienced leader who…" is banned in documents. "I built a 250-person organisation in twelve months" is the form. The reader derives the identity; the document does not announce it.

**One licensed exception.** Recruiter outreach and cover letters may open "I am a twenty-year operations and technology executive" or its equivalent, because that genre expects it. The licence covers that genre only.

## Order by weight, never by time

A profile opens on the largest true claim for this seat and works outward only as far as the story needs. Chronology is not an argument. Nobody hires a senior candidate because of where they started; the origin appears, if at all, as one clause of range near the end.

## Banned families

**The Kinnock construction.** "Not X but Y." "Not because A but because B." "Rather than X, Y." Balanced antithesis of any kind. State Y.

**The fronted noun phrase.** "The X is what I Y." "Scaling is the work I have done." "Operations is my ground." Say "I scaled…", "I ran operations…".

**Manufactured rhythm.** Tricolons assembled for cadence, pairs balanced for sound, one-line paragraphs placed as punches, aphorisms and slogans. A list of three is permitted only when there are exactly three real things.

**The ablative absolute.** Participial or "with"-led clauses hanging off a main clause: "having run…", "with quality held…", "the work done, we moved on." Every action gets a subject and a finite verb.

**Mannered prose.** Metaphor and flourish in place of direct statement: "a dial worth turning" for "a parameter worth varying", "earns its keep" for "matters". The phrases display the writer, drag in connotations the writer did not choose, and make the reader work so the writer can perform. When a literal phrase is available, use it.

**Self-certifying adjectives.** "Proven", "strong", "extensive", "genuinely", "world-class", "exceptional". The receipt proves; the adjective pleads.

**Agentless metonymy.** "The platform scaled." "Attrition fell." Say who did it.

**Assistant vocabulary.** "Leverage", "drive", "seamless", "robust", "passionate", "journey", "adjacent" and "adjacency", "ecosystem" as a synonym for a vendor's products, and any word the person has said they hate. Name the tool, name the relation.

**Echo of the job description.** Do not repeat the posting's phrases back as your own. A reader who wrote the posting recognises the echo and reads it as a candidate with nothing of their own. Answer the requirement with a receipt in different words. Naming a public practice by its public name, with a receipt attached, is not an echo.

**Commute logistics.** No journey times in any document. Location is a place name, stated only where the posting makes location a condition. The one exception is a role demanding full-time presence at a non-metropolitan site, where the distance is the argument and may be stated once.

## Composition fraud

The gravest fault, because it survives fact-checking and fails in the interview. True atoms are assembled into a molecule that says something the person never did. "Ran production operations on AWS, Docker and Kafka": each element true, and the sentence claims a platform-engineering role that was never held. The honest form states the relation: "my organisation provided 24/7 production support for a platform built on…". Test every composed claim, not its components. Corollaries: a requirement-coverage audit is a diagnostic, never a build plan; spoken closers do not transplant into prose; technician-level facts reprice a board-level document; volunteer credentials are not domain experience.

## Profile precision

Headline claims match the body at the same size. If the cell says the organisation sat inside a parent's centre, the profile says so too. Naming the institution makes a claim smaller and more credible at once. A careful reader reconciles profile against body, and any gap reads as inflation.

## Numbers once

Every figure appears exactly once in a document. The profile takes the headline figures; the career cells take the operational ones; capability blocks carry no numbers. A number told twice reads as a thin record padded.

## Economy is not terseness

Compression past readability fails the reader's "what does this mean, slow down." Receipts get enough words to be understood on first reading. Business letters use normal business phrasing: "I am very happy to travel throughout Europe as needed", never "Europe travel is routine." Defects are not volunteered in letters; state the adjacent record affirmatively and leave gaps for questions. A form with a knockout question is different: answer it truthfully, then give the nearest true receipt.

## The instance and the pattern

A thing done daily for years is stated as the pattern, never as a single remembered instance. "Diagnosed a container problem" reads as one afternoon; "diagnosed platform faults as the daily work of the role" is the truth and the stronger claim. Conversely, a single day's work is never reported as an achievement.

## The invented gap

Do not confess a gap the reader has not asked about, and do not fill it with costume. If a rival for the seat would plausibly hold the missing experience, silence is correct; if a form asks directly, truth is correct. Volunteer credentials, hobbies and adjacent qualifications never dress up as domain experience.

## Certifications are priced per reader

A badge is currency at one altitude and costume at another. Technician certifications belong on technician-level documents and off executive ones; vendor sales badges belong nowhere; a professional chartership belongs in the header. Keep a written ruling for every credential the person holds and apply it per document.

## Length

Length is never the constraint; content is. A document travelling on a warm introduction may run long if every sentence is a receipt. Filler, and words that do no work, are cut at any length.

## Failure modes recorded in practice

These devices returned after being banned, in a real search, and were caught by the person and not the assistant. Every paragraph opener is checked against this list before a document leaves.

- The identity opener regrows in new syntax: "Senior executive with 20 years" becomes "I am an executive who…" becomes "I run the machinery underneath." Same offence.
- The fronted noun phrase returns under momentum, especially when a paragraph tries to answer a requirement directly.
- The hero's journey returns whenever the writer reaches for narrative: the first job, the humble start, the origin story.
- Em dashes return in every long draft.
- Journey times and travel logistics creep into profiles as helpfulness.
- Volunteer credentials creep into profiles as domain fit.
- The mannered phrase returns as warmth: "earns its place", "does the work", "home ground".

The only reliable defence is the re-read: the whole law, every build, and every paragraph opener checked against this list before the document leaves.
